## Directions a matrix only stretches

Most vectors change direction when a matrix acts on them. A few special ones don't: they only get stretched. Those are the **eigenvectors**, and the stretch factors are the **eigenvalues**:

$$
A v = \lambda v, \qquad v \neq 0
$$

Open the matrix map from Lesson 2 below and choose "symmetric": the purple dashed lines are the eigenvector directions, lines that map onto themselves. "Rotate 30°" has none (its eigenvalues are complex), and "shear" has only one.

<Viz name="MatrixMap" />

For a 2 × 2 matrix the eigenvalues solve $\lambda^2 - \operatorname{tr}(A)\,\lambda + \det A = 0$. Two facts hold in any size: the eigenvalues add up to the **trace** (the sum of the diagonal) and multiply to the **determinant**. In code, never go through that polynomial for anything bigger than a toy: root-finding on a characteristic polynomial is numerically fragile. Library eigensolvers use orthogonal transformations instead.

## Change to the matrix's own coordinates

If $A$ has a full set of eigenvectors, put them in the columns of $V$ and the eigenvalues on the diagonal of $\Lambda$. Then $AV = V\Lambda$, so

$$
A = V \Lambda V^{-1}, \qquad A^k = V \Lambda^k V^{-1}
$$

In eigenvector coordinates the matrix is just a set of independent stretches, and powers of it are powers of numbers. Write any starting vector as $x_0 = c_1 v_1 + \dots + c_n v_n$, and after $k$ steps of $x_{k+1} = A x_k$:

$$
x_k = c_1 \lambda_1^k v_1 + c_2 \lambda_2^k v_2 + \dots + c_n \lambda_n^k v_n
$$

**Symmetric matrices are the best case**, and they're everywhere: covariance matrices, conductance and stiffness matrices, $X^\top X$, Hessians. The **spectral theorem** says a real symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues and orthonormal eigenvectors, so $A = Q \Lambda Q^\top$ with $Q^\top Q = I$. Use `np.linalg.eigh` (or `eigvalsh`) for them: it's faster than `eig`, more accurate, and returns real results sorted in ascending order.

## Stability is about eigenvalues

That expansion answers Dev's controller question directly. Each mode is multiplied by $\lambda_i$ every step, so:

- **It settles** if every $|\lambda_i| < 1$ (the spectral radius is below 1), at a rate set by the largest $|\lambda_i|$. After 20 steps, a mode with $\lambda = 0.9$ has shrunk to 0.12 of its size, while one with $\lambda = 0.5$ is down to 10⁻⁶: the slowest mode is what you see.
- **It runs away** if any $|\lambda_i| > 1$.
- **It oscillates** when eigenvalues come in complex pairs $r e^{\pm i\theta}$: the mode rotates by $\theta$ per step while its size scales by $r$. For example, $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0.95 & 0.10 \\ -0.20 & 0.90 \end{bmatrix}$ has eigenvalues $0.925 \pm 0.139i$, magnitude 0.935: a damped oscillation with a period of about 42 steps.

Continuous time works the same way with exponentials: $\dot x = A x$ has modes $e^{\lambda_i t}$, stable when every eigenvalue has a negative real part.

Dev's pump trip is the continuous case. Give every node of the cold-plate stack a heat capacity, collected in a diagonal matrix $C$; with Lesson 2's conductance matrix $K$, deviations from steady state obey

$$
C\,\dot T = -K\,T
$$

Its modes decay like $e^{-\lambda t}$, where $\lambda$ are the eigenvalues of $C^{-1}K$, and the **time constants** are $\tau = 1/\lambda$. For the junction–case–plate stack, they're about 0.79 s, 2.65 s and 12.4 s: a fast jump at the junction and a slow creep set by the plate's heat capacity. That's the two-speed rise Dev saw.

Simulating $x \leftarrow Ax$ also gives you an algorithm. Normalize after every multiplication and the dominant eigenvector takes over, with the error shrinking like $|\lambda_2/\lambda_1|^k$. That's **power iteration**, the simplest eigensolver, and the idea behind PageRank and behind the randomized SVD you'll meet in the next lesson.

## Quadratic forms and positive definiteness

A symmetric matrix also defines a **quadratic form**, a number for every vector:

$$
f(x) = x^\top A x
$$

These measure energy and spread throughout engineering and ML: strain energy $\tfrac{1}{2}u^\top K u$, the variance $w^\top \Sigma w$ of a weighted combination of variables, the curvature of a loss near its minimum. In eigenvector coordinates $y = Q^\top x$ it splits into independent squares:

$$
x^\top A x = \lambda_1 y_1^2 + \lambda_2 y_2^2 + \dots + \lambda_n y_n^2
$$

So the eigenvalues decide its shape:

- all $\lambda_i > 0$: **positive definite**, a bowl with a single lowest point, and $x^\top A x > 0$ for every $x \neq 0$;
- all $\lambda_i \ge 0$: **positive semidefinite**, a bowl with flat directions (every covariance matrix, and $X^\top X$);
- mixed signs: **indefinite**, a saddle.

The level sets $x^\top A x = 1$ of a positive definite matrix are ellipses with axes along the eigenvectors and semi-axes $1/\sqrt{\lambda_i}$. The ratio $\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$ is the condition number, and it measures how elongated the bowl is. Module 3 shows why that ratio decides how slowly gradient descent crawls along a long, narrow valley.

<Craft title="Eigen habits worth keeping">
- `np.linalg.eigh` or `eigvalsh` for symmetric matrices; `eig` only when you must, and expect complex output and eigenvectors that aren't orthogonal.
- Test positive definiteness with `np.linalg.cholesky` (it raises `LinAlgError` if the matrix isn't), or with `eigvalsh` and a tolerance for semidefinite cases.
- Eigenvalues tell you where a system ends up, not everything on the way there. A non-symmetric matrix can amplify a state before it decays: `[[0.5, 4], [0, 0.5]]` has both eigenvalues at 0.5, yet it stretches (0, 1) to a vector about 4 times as long in one step. Check transient growth in controllers, not just the spectral radius.
- Simulating a stiff system? Use an implicit or exponential integrator, or keep explicit steps below 2/λ_max.
- For a generalized problem $Kv = \lambda Cv$ with symmetric $K$ and positive definite $C$, use `scipy.linalg.eigh(K, C)`.
</Craft>
