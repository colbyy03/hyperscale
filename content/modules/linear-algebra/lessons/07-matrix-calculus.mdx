## The gradient, defined by what it does

For a scalar function of a vector, $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$, the gradient $\nabla f(w)$ is the vector that predicts how $f$ changes under any small step $d$:

$$
f(w + d) \approx f(w) + \nabla f(w) \cdot d
$$

Two consequences follow from Lesson 1. The gradient has the **same shape as $w$**, which makes shapes a free check on every derivation. And among all steps of a given length, the change is largest when $d$ points along the gradient, because a dot product is largest for parallel vectors: the gradient is the direction of steepest ascent.

This definition is also the most reliable way to derive a gradient: expand $f(w + d)$, keep the terms linear in $d$, and read off what multiplies $d$.

## Four rules that cover most of ML

| Function | Gradient | Where it shows up |
|---|---|---|
| $a^\top w$ | $a$ | every linear model |
| $w^\top A w$ | $(A + A^\top)\,w$, which is $2Aw$ for symmetric $A$ | variances, energies, penalties |
| $\tfrac{1}{2}\|Xw - y\|^2$ | $X^\top(Xw - y)$ | least squares |
| $\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\|w\|^2$ | $\lambda w$ | ridge regularization, weight decay |

The third rule is the **chain rule** in matrix form. The loss depends on $w$ only through the residual $r = Xw - y$. A step $d$ in $w$ moves the residual by $Xd$, and ½‖r‖² changes by $r \cdot Xd = (X^\top r) \cdot d$. The transpose appears because you're carrying a change *backwards* through $X$, from the residual to the weights. That's the whole idea of backpropagation, which Module 11 builds into an engine.

The **Hessian**, the matrix of second derivatives, describes the curvature. For least squares it's $X^\top X$ (plus $\lambda I$ with ridge): a quadratic form, exactly as in Lesson 4. Positive definite means a bowl with one minimum, and the ratio of its largest to smallest eigenvalue is how elongated the bowl is. Module 3 picks up from here: that ratio decides how slowly gradient descent zigzags to the bottom, and ridge's $\lambda I$ lifts every eigenvalue, which makes the bowl rounder as well as the solution unique.

<Bridge>
You've used this rule in structures. The strain energy of a linear elastic structure is the quadratic form $U = \tfrac{1}{2}u^\top K u$, with a symmetric stiffness matrix. Its gradient with respect to the displacements is $Ku$, the internal force vector, which is the second row of the table with the ½ cancelling the 2. Setting the gradient of the total potential energy $\tfrac{1}{2}u^\top Ku - f^\top u$ to zero gives $Ku = f$: the minimum-energy principle is a matrix-calculus statement.
</Bridge>

## Check your gradients numerically

Derivations go wrong in small ways: a sign, a transpose, a missing ½. So before trusting a hand-coded gradient, compare it with a finite-difference estimate. For each coordinate:

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial w_i} \approx \frac{f(w + h e_i) - f(w - h e_i)}{2h}
$$

This **central difference** cancels the second-order term of the Taylor expansion, so its error shrinks like $h^2$. A one-sided difference, $(f(w + he_i) - f(w))/h$, has error proportional to $h$. Rounding pushes the other way: subtracting two nearly equal function values loses digits, and that error grows like $\varepsilon/h$. The measured trade-off, on $f(w) = \sum_i e^{w_i}$ (the rounding-dominated entries vary from run to run):

| Step $h$ | 10⁻³ | 10⁻⁵ | 10⁻⁸ | 10⁻¹² |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Central difference, relative error | 2 × 10⁻⁷ | about 10⁻¹¹ | about 10⁻⁸ | about 10⁻⁴ |
| One-sided difference, relative error | 5 × 10⁻⁴ | 5 × 10⁻⁶ | about 10⁻⁸ | about 10⁻⁴ |

So use central differences with $h$ around 10⁻⁵ in float64, and compare with a **relative** error, $\|g - g_{\text{num}}\| / \max(\|g\|, \|g_{\text{num}}\|)$. A correct gradient comes in around 10⁻¹¹; the classic bugs come in near 1 (a flipped sign gives exactly 2, a doubled gradient 0.5). Anything below 10⁻⁶ passes.

<Craft title="Gradient habits worth keeping">
- Write the shape next to every term of a derivation. The gradient must have the parameter's shape.
- Test with non-square, random inputs (an 8 × 3 matrix, not 3 × 3), so a missing transpose can't hide behind matching shapes.
- Check in float64, at a random point rather than a special one such as zero, where many wrong gradients happen to agree.
- For a model with millions of parameters, check a few random directions: compare $\nabla f \cdot d$ with $(f(w + hd) - f(w - hd))/2h$.
- Autograd (Modules 11 and 13) computes gradients for you, but custom operations and fused kernels still need exactly this check.
</Craft>
