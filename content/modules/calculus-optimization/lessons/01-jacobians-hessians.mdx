## From gradient to Jacobian

Module 2 defined the gradient of a scalar function by what it does: $f(x + d) \approx f(x) + \nabla f \cdot d$. Most functions in ML and engineering return vectors: a model's residuals at every data point, a softmax's probabilities, a robot arm's position. For $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$, the **Jacobian** $J$ plays the gradient's role:

$$
f(x + d) \approx f(x) + J\,d, \qquad J_{ij} = \frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_j}
$$

$J$ is $m \times n$: **one row per output, one column per input**. Row $i$ is the gradient of output $i$. Column $j$ says how every output moves when input $j$ moves. Noor's solver needs exactly this: the Jacobian of the residual vector (one residual per meter reading) with respect to the three model parameters, a tall $m \times 3$ matrix.

The chain rule becomes a matrix product. If $h(x) = f(g(x))$, then

$$
J_h(x) = J_f(g(x))\, J_g(x)
$$

and the shapes chain like Module 2's composition of maps: $(m \times k)(k \times n)$. Every gradient a framework computes is a long product of such Jacobians. The order in which you multiply them makes an enormous difference to the cost, which is the subject of the next lesson.

The softmax is worth knowing by heart. Its Jacobian is $\partial p_i / \partial z_j = p_i(\delta_{ij} - p_j)$, or $\operatorname{diag}(p) - pp^\top$, and combined with cross-entropy it gives the cleanest gradient in machine learning.

## The Hessian: curvature

The **Hessian** $H$ is the matrix of second derivatives, $H_{ij} = \partial^2 f / \partial x_i \partial x_j$, which is also the Jacobian of the gradient. For smooth functions it's symmetric, and it completes the local picture of $f$ as a quadratic model:

$$
f(x + d) \approx f(x) + g^\top d + \tfrac{1}{2}\, d^\top H d
$$

That's Module 2's quadratic form, now describing the shape of a loss near a point. At a critical point, where $g = 0$, the eigenvalues of $H$ classify it: all positive is a minimum, all negative a maximum, mixed signs a **saddle**. In high dimensions saddles are far more common than local minima: it takes every one of thousands of eigenvalues being positive to make a minimum, and mixed signs are the typical case.

For least squares there's a useful approximation. With residuals $r(\theta)$ and their Jacobian $J$, the Hessian of $\tfrac{1}{2}\|r\|^2$ is $J^\top J$ plus terms weighted by the residuals themselves, which are small near a good fit:

$$
H \approx J^\top J \qquad \text{(the Gauss–Newton approximation)}
$$

This answers Noor's second question. Directions where $J^\top J$ has small eigenvalues are directions in parameter space where the fit barely changes, so the data barely determine them: the conditioning story from Module 2, seen from the loss. Module 4 turns that curvature into standard errors.

## Checking derivatives numerically

Every hand-derived Jacobian deserves a check before an optimizer trusts it. Central differences estimate it one column at a time: nudge input $j$ by $\pm h$ and divide the change in every output by $2h$. That costs $2n$ evaluations of $f$, which is cheap for checking and too expensive for a model with a million parameters.

Second derivatives can be checked the same way, one level up. A **Hessian-vector product** needs only two gradient evaluations:

$$
H v \approx \frac{\nabla f(x + \varepsilon v) - \nabla f(x - \varepsilon v)}{2\varepsilon}
$$

without ever forming $H$. Newton–CG methods and tools that measure the curvature of neural-network losses are built on that trick.

<Bridge>
Nonlinear finite-element analysis lives on Jacobians. The **tangent stiffness matrix** is the Jacobian of the internal force vector with respect to the nodal displacements, and each Newton–Raphson iteration of a nonlinear solve uses it to correct the displacements. When convergence stalls, a wrong tangent is the usual suspect: the same failure as Noor's hand-coded Jacobian, which still "converges", just badly or to the wrong place.
</Bridge>

<Craft title="Derivative habits worth keeping">
- Use the m × n convention (rows are outputs), and write shapes next to every factor in a chain-rule product.
- Check every hand-written Jacobian or gradient with central differences at a random point before passing it to an optimizer.
- Scale the difference step to the variable: a parameter of size 10⁻⁴ needs a much smaller absolute step than one of size 10⁴.
- Prefer automatic differentiation (the next lesson) to hand derivation once a model has more than a few parameters, and still spot-check it.
- When a fit "converges" to implausible parameters, suspect the derivatives before the data.
</Craft>
