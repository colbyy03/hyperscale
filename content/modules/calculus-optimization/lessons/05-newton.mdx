## Newton's method: jump to the bottom of the model

Lesson 1's second-order Taylor model, $f(w + d) \approx f + g^\top d + \tfrac{1}{2} d^\top H d$, is a quadratic in $d$, and its minimum is where its gradient vanishes, $g + Hd = 0$. **Newton's method** solves for that step and takes it:

$$
H\,d = -g, \qquad w \leftarrow w + d
$$

(solved with `np.linalg.solve`, never by inverting $H$). Three properties make it remarkable:

- **One step on a quadratic.** The model is exact, so Newton lands on the minimum immediately.
- **Quadratic convergence.** Near a minimum the error is roughly squared at each step: the number of correct digits doubles. That's the 9 × 10⁻⁵ → 7 × 10⁻¹¹ step in Ines's run.
- **Affine invariance.** Rescale a feature by 1,000 and Newton's iterates rescale with it: the path and the iteration count don't change. Gradient descent's step depends on the units; Newton's doesn't. That's why Ines's raw features don't bother it.

## When the quadratic model lies

Newton trusts its local model completely, and far from a minimum the model can be badly wrong. Two failures matter in practice:

- **Overshooting.** On the banana-shaped Rosenbrock function, pure Newton from the standard start converges in 7 iterations, but one of its full steps *raises* the loss on the way.
- **Saddles and negative curvature.** If $H$ isn't positive definite, the Newton step heads for the nearest point where the gradient vanishes, which may be a saddle or even a maximum. Lesson 3's explorer showed pure Newton walking straight onto the saddle.

The standard fixes are a **line search** and a guard on the direction. Take the Newton direction $d$, check that it points downhill ($g^\top d < 0$, otherwise fall back to $-g$, or add a multiple of $I$ to $H$), then shrink the step until it makes real progress. The **Armijo condition** accepts a step size α when

$$
f(w + \alpha d) \le f(w) + c\,\alpha\, g^\top d
$$

with a small $c$ such as 10⁻⁴: the decrease must be at least a fraction of what the slope promised. Backtracking halves α until that holds. Damped Newton with Armijo backtracking solves Rosenbrock in 22 iterations without ever going uphill; gradient descent with the same line search needs 13,756.

## Least squares: Gauss–Newton and Levenberg–Marquardt

For nonlinear least squares, such as Noor's chiller model from Lesson 1, the Hessian of $\tfrac{1}{2}\|r(\theta)\|^2$ is approximately $J^\top J$. Using that approximation in Newton's step gives **Gauss–Newton**, which needs only the Jacobian of the residuals:

$$
(J^\top J)\, d = -J^\top r
$$

**Levenberg–Marquardt** adds a damping term, $(J^\top J + \lambda I)\, d = -J^\top r$: with large λ it takes short gradient-descent-like steps, with small λ it's Gauss–Newton, and λ adapts as the fit improves. It's ridge regression applied to the step. `scipy.optimize.least_squares` implements it (`method="lm"`) alongside a trust-region method (`"trf"`) that handles bounds.

## Quasi-Newton: curvature from gradients

Newton's method needs the Hessian ($n^2$ numbers) and a linear solve ($\sim n^3$ operations) at every step. **Quasi-Newton** methods build an approximation from information they already have. Each step produces a change in position $s = w_{k+1} - w_k$ and a change in gradient $y = g_{k+1} - g_k$, and a curvature matrix $B$ consistent with them must satisfy the **secant condition** $B s = y$. **BFGS** updates its approximation (of $H^{-1}$, so no solve is needed) to satisfy it at every step, and converges superlinearly near the minimum. **L-BFGS** keeps only the last few (s, y) pairs, typically 5 to 20, so its memory and cost per step grow linearly in $n$: it handles millions of parameters.

That's what Ines's "lbfgs" was doing: 87 iterations on her raw features, against more than a million for gradient descent. L-BFGS isn't fully affine invariant, though: on standardized features it needed 9, so scale anyway.

<Bridge>
Nonlinear finite-element solvers are Newton's method at scale: the tangent stiffness matrix from Lesson 1 is the Hessian of the potential energy, and each Newton–Raphson iteration solves with it. The failures are the same too. Near a limit point, where a structure is about to snap through or buckle, the tangent stiffness loses positive definiteness and plain Newton fails, which is why FE codes use line searches and arc-length methods. Quasi-Newton updates of the stiffness (BFGS among them) are a standard option for saving the cost of reassembling it every iteration.
</Bridge>

## When second-order methods pay

| Situation | Good choice |
|---|---|
| Small or medium smooth problem (up to a few thousand parameters): logistic regression, model calibration | Newton or damped Newton; quadratic convergence in a handful of steps |
| Smooth deterministic problem with up to millions of parameters | L-BFGS (`scipy.optimize.minimize(method="L-BFGS-B")`) |
| Nonlinear least squares | Gauss–Newton or Levenberg–Marquardt (`scipy.optimize.least_squares`) |
| Large neural network trained on mini-batches | SGD with momentum or AdamW (Lesson 4): noisy gradients make curvature estimates unreliable and expensive |

<Craft title="Second-order habits worth keeping">
- Solve with the Hessian (`np.linalg.solve`, or a Cholesky factorization when it's positive definite); never invert it.
- Guard every Newton step: check the direction is downhill, and use a line search or a trust region.
- Add a small ridge term when a problem might be separable or rank deficient, so the minimizer exists and the Hessian stays invertible.
- Reach for library solvers first (`least_squares`, `minimize` with L-BFGS-B, scikit-learn's `lbfgs`), and pass them correct derivatives (Lesson 1's checker).
- Standardize features even for L-BFGS: it isn't fully scale invariant, and well-scaled problems converge in fewer iterations.
</Craft>
