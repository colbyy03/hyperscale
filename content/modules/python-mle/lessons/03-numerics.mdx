## Floating point is a finite ruler

A float stores a sign, an exponent and a fixed number of significant bits: 53 for `float64` (about 16 decimal digits) and 24 for `float32` (about 7). The gap between 1 and the next representable number is **machine epsilon**, about 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁶ for `float64` and 1.2 × 10⁻⁷ for `float32`. In `float32`, `1 + 10⁻⁸` is exactly `1`.

Three consequences matter every week in ML work:

1. **Rounding happens on every operation**, so `0.1 + 0.2 == 0.3` is `False`. Compare floats with a tolerance (`np.isclose`, `pytest.approx`), never with `==`.
2. **Addition isn't associative**: `(0.1 + 0.2) + 0.3` and `0.1 + (0.2 + 0.3)` differ in the last bit. The order of a sum changes its result, which is why parallel and GPU reductions aren't bit-for-bit reproducible.
3. **Subtracting nearly equal numbers destroys digits.** If two numbers agree in their first 15 digits, their difference has at most one or two digits that mean anything. This is *catastrophic cancellation*.

## Two cures for cancellation

**Center first.** The variance only depends on deviations from the mean, so compute those: two passes (mean first, then the mean of squared deviations) are stable. That is what `np.var` does.

**Or update as you go.** When data streams past once (a sensor feed, a dataset too large for memory), Welford's method keeps a count $n$, the running mean $\bar{x}$, and $M_2$, the sum of squared deviations. For each new value $x$:

$$
n \leftarrow n + 1, \quad \delta = x - \bar{x}, \quad \bar{x} \leftarrow \bar{x} + \frac{\delta}{n}, \quad M_2 \leftarrow M_2 + \delta\,(x - \bar{x})
$$

and the variance is $M_2 / n$. Every quantity it accumulates is small, so nothing cancels.

## Overflow and underflow: log-sum-exp

`np.exp` overflows to `inf` above about 709 in `float64`, and above 88 in `float32`. Softmax turns scores into probabilities, $\mathrm{softmax}(x)_i = e^{x_i} / \sum_j e^{x_j}$, and with scores of 1000 the naive formula computes inf / inf = NaN. That's Ines's second bug.

The fix rests on one identity. Subtracting the same constant $m$ from every score changes nothing:

$$
\log \sum_i e^{x_i} = m + \log \sum_i e^{x_i - m}
$$

Choose $m = \max_i x_i$. Then the largest exponent is $e^0 = 1$, nothing overflows, and the sum is at least 1, so its logarithm is safe too. Softmax gets the same shift. This **log-sum-exp trick** appears in every cross-entropy loss, every log-likelihood, and every attention layer you'll write.

<Craft title="Where these bite in production">
- Losses computed in `float32` or `bfloat16` on accelerators. Frameworks fuse log-softmax and cross-entropy precisely to apply this trick, so use the fused version rather than `log(softmax(x))`.
- Feature statistics computed in one pass on a GPU or a stream. Use Welford-style updates, or center first.
- Timestamps in seconds since 1970, about 1.8 × 10⁹. In `float32` you lose minute-level resolution. Keep time as integers or in `float64`.
- Tests that compare floats with `==` pass on your laptop and fail in CI on a different CPU. Always compare with a tolerance.
</Craft>

## Measure, then optimize

Making code fast starts with measuring where the time goes, because intuition is usually wrong about it.

- **`timeit`** times a small snippet many times and reports the best run: `python -m timeit -s "import numpy as np; x = np.random.rand(10**6)" "x.sum()"`, or `%timeit` in a notebook.
- **`cProfile`** shows which functions consume the time in a whole program: `python -m cProfile -s cumtime pipeline.py`. Sort by cumulative time and read from the top.
- **Line-level and memory tools** (`line_profiler`, `tracemalloc`, `memray`) come out once the profile has pointed you at a function.

Then work down the usual list: a better algorithm; vectorizing the hot loop; avoiding needless copies and temporaries; smaller dtypes; and only then compiling (Numba) or parallelizing. After each change, check that results still match within a stated tolerance. A 10× speedup that changes the answer is a bug.

<Bridge>
Profiling is an energy audit for code. You'd never retrofit a chiller plant before metering it, because the losses are rarely where you'd guess. The same goes for a slow pipeline: meter first, fix the biggest loss, then meter again.
